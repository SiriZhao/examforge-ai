# Introduction to Plant Biology

> Fictional demo course material for CampusForge testing. This is not based on any real course, textbook, lecture, or exam.

## Lecture 1: What Makes a Plant a Plant?

Plants are multicellular eukaryotes that capture light energy through photosynthesis. Most land plants share several features:

- Cell walls containing cellulose.
- Chloroplasts that contain chlorophyll pigments.
- Alternation of generations in the life cycle.
- Specialized tissues for transport, support, and reproduction.

The central challenge of plant life on land is balance: plants must collect sunlight and carbon dioxide while preventing excessive water loss.

## Lecture 2: Photosynthesis and Energy Flow

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy. The simplified overall reaction is:

```text
carbon dioxide + water + light energy -> glucose + oxygen
```

The process has two major stages:

1. Light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and NADPH.
2. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma and uses carbon dioxide to build sugar molecules.

Important exam distinction:

- Photosystem II splits water and releases oxygen.
- Photosystem I helps reduce NADP+ to NADPH.
- The Calvin cycle does not directly require light, but it depends on ATP and NADPH from the light reactions.

## Lecture 3: Plant Cells and Tissues

Plant cells often contain a large central vacuole, chloroplasts, and a rigid cell wall. These structures help plants maintain shape, store molecules, and capture light.

Three tissue systems appear throughout the plant body:

- Dermal tissue protects the outer surface.
- Ground tissue performs photosynthesis, storage, and support.
- Vascular tissue transports water, minerals, and sugars.

Vascular tissue includes:

- Xylem: moves water and dissolved minerals, usually from roots to shoots.
- Phloem: moves sugars and signaling molecules between sources and sinks.

## Lecture 4: Roots, Stems, and Leaves

Roots anchor the plant, absorb water and minerals, and often store carbohydrates. Root hairs increase surface area for absorption.

Stems support leaves and flowers while connecting roots to shoots through vascular bundles.

Leaves are optimized for photosynthesis:

- The cuticle reduces water loss.
- Stomata regulate gas exchange.
- Guard cells open and close stomata.
- Mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts.

Trade-off to remember: opening stomata allows carbon dioxide to enter, but it also increases water loss through transpiration.

## Lecture 5: Plant Hormones and Responses

Plant hormones coordinate growth and responses to environmental signals.

| Hormone | Simplified role |
| --- | --- |
| Auxin | Cell elongation, apical dominance, phototropism |
| Cytokinin | Cell division, shoot development |
| Gibberellin | Stem elongation, seed germination |
| Abscisic acid | Drought response, stomatal closure |
| Ethylene | Fruit ripening, stress responses |

Phototropism is directional growth toward light. In a simplified model, auxin accumulates on the shaded side of a stem, causing those cells to elongate more and bend the shoot toward light.

## Lecture 6: Reproduction and Seeds

Flowers are reproductive structures in angiosperms. Pollination transfers pollen to the stigma. Fertilization occurs when sperm cells reach the ovule.

Seeds protect embryos and provide stored resources. Fruits help protect seeds and support dispersal by wind, water, or animals.

Key vocabulary:

- Pollination: transfer of pollen.
- Fertilization: fusion of gametes.
- Germination: early growth of a seed into a seedling.
- Dormancy: delayed growth under unfavorable conditions.

High-priority topics:

- Major plant hormones and their functions.
- Pollination, fertilization, and seed formation.
